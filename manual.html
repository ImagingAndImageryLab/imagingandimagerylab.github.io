Lab Manual

How we work, what we expect from each other, and where things live. Lab members: internal access ↓

This manual is a working document. It is adapted from the lab manual of the Imagine Reality Lab at UCL, which in turn drew on manuals by Steve Fleming, Roy Salomon, Anne Urai and Mariam Aly. If something here does not match your experience, say so — the manual should serve the lab, not the other way round.

Welcome

Welcome to the Imaging & Imagery Lab. We study how the brain builds the world we call reality: how imagination and perception meet, how an image is generated without any light reaching the eye, and what that tells us about conscious experience. Our tools are brain imaging, EEG, TMS and psychophysics.

The lab is small and student-led. That means two things. First, you will not be handed a ready-made project and told exactly what to do — you will help shape the question. Second, the lab depends on people being generous with their time: explaining something you already understand to someone who does not is part of the work, not a distraction from it.

Getting started

Within your first week, try to do these:

  • Read this manual.
  • Meet the Lab Director to be added to the lab group chat and calendar.
  • Write a short biography (and, if you like, a photo) for the People page.
  • Create a GitHub account — code and materials live there.
  • Read the background papers below.
  • Fix a regular meeting slot with your supervisor.

Vision and values

Doing science well

Doing science is a privilege and a responsibility. We follow the usual standards of research integrity — reliability, honesty, respect and accountability — and we should always be able to explain the reasoning behind a decision, and to change our minds when the reasoning does not hold.

  • Make the work accessible. Preprint when you can, publish open access when you can, and discuss the venue with your supervisor before submitting.
  • Report transparently. Never oversell a finding or hide an unexpected one. If the data do not show what you hoped, that is information, not failure — it is why the study was worth running.
  • Write code you could hand to someone else. A pipeline that runs from raw data to the figure, with comments, saves you more time than it costs.
  • Share data. Inside the lab, share freely. Outside, we aim to release data when the paper is published, unless there is a reason we cannot (for example, data that cannot be anonymised).

Keeping an open mind

Once an explanation sounds good, it is tempting to look only for evidence that supports it. We try to resist that: question each other, propose alternative explanations, and design the test that could prove us wrong. Do it in a way that makes the science better rather than the person smaller.

Culture

Curiosity-driven work is worth doing for its own sake, and it is more fun with people who care about it. We want a lab where people from different backgrounds can follow their curiosity, ask basic questions without embarrassment, and disagree productively. In return we ask for real commitment to the research and to keeping the culture healthy.

Well-being

Happy scientists do better science. Work-life balance is not a concession to be earned; it is what makes sustained work possible. Take breaks, keep something outside the lab, and tell someone early if the workload is getting out of hand — we routinely underestimate how long things take.

Research is also unusually exposed to uncertainty, and the feedback that your work is going somewhere often arrives months late. Feeling like an impostor is common and says very little about your actual ability. If you are struggling, talk to the Lab Director or your supervisor in confidence; the university’s counselling service is also available and does not require you to explain yourself to us first.

Diversity and respect

We want the lab to be a place where people from different backgrounds can do their best work. Beyond fairness, diverse teams ask better questions and catch each other’s blind spots.

There is zero tolerance for harassment, sexism, racism, homophobia, transphobia or any other form of bigotry. If you experience or witness misconduct, tell the Principal Investigator or the Lab Director. If the concern involves one of them, take it to the department or to the university’s relevant office.

Expectations and responsibilities

Everyone in the lab is expected to:

  • Work on something you care about, and do it in a way you would be happy to defend.
  • Be supportive of colleagues — we are a team, and the lab is only as good as its weakest week.
  • Keep up with the field and suggest improvements to what we do, whether in methods, analysis, open science or culture.
  • Be open about mistakes. You will make them; hiding them is what turns a mistake into a problem. Nobody will be shamed for saying “this is wrong, here is how I found out”.
  • Try to prevent mistakes in the first place: do not rush, check twice, and ask when something looks off.
  • Know the limits of your knowledge, and do not be afraid to ask — or to respectfully disagree with someone more senior, including your supervisor.
  • Judge when to keep trying and when to ask for help. If you know the next step, take it; if you can no longer see one, stop and ask rather than losing days.
  • Take an active role in your own development, and say what kind of career you are aiming at.

General policies

Authorship

We follow the APA guidelines: authorship credit should reflect the individual’s contribution to the study. We discuss authorship at the start of a project, not at the end. The person leading a project is normally first author, and the supervising PI normally last. If roles change later, we revisit the question explicitly rather than letting it drift.

Working hours and presence

There are no fixed hours, and no one is counting them. Being in the lab during weekday daytime still matters: it is how people learn from each other, and most of the ideas that move a project forward come out of unplanned conversations. Lab meetings are in person. If you need a different arrangement for a period, say so early.

Communication

Day-to-day coordination happens in the lab group chat and by email; announcements, papers and resources are shared there too. Check it once a day on weekdays. You are not expected to answer outside working hours, and neither is anyone else.

Communication styles differ across cultures and backgrounds, and most misunderstandings come from assumptions rather than bad intent. If something someone said landed badly, ask what they meant before concluding.

Meetings

You will have a regular one-to-one with your supervisor — typically a fixed weekly slot. Some weeks a ten-minute check-in is enough; some weeks you need the whole hour. Come with the things you want to discuss, including what is stuck.

The lab meeting is where work in progress gets shown, including unfinished work. Presenting something half-done is normal and useful; presenting nothing for months is what we are trying to avoid. Department seminars and the university’s talk series are worth attending when the topic is close to your work.

Programming

We mostly use MATLAB (SPM, FieldTrip) for imaging and electrophysiology, and jsPsych for online experiments. Python and R are welcome where they fit better. The rule is that someone else in the lab should be able to run your code from the repository: keep it documented, keep it reproducible, and put it on GitHub.

Deadlines

Academia has few fixed deadlines, which makes it easy for things to drift. If you work better with explicit checkpoints, say so and we will set them. If a deadline you agreed to is no longer realistic, say that early rather than at the deadline.

Conferences

Conferences are how you meet the field and find out what is being done before it is published. We aim for one or two a year depending on the project stage. Talk to your supervisor before planning. Venues that usually fit our work include:

  • Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness (ASSC)
  • Vision Sciences Society (VSS)
  • European Conference on Visual Perception (ECVP)
  • Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience (CCN)
  • International Conference on Cognitive Neuroscience (ICON)

Thank you
